Why we built it

A tipping comp is already a community. It was just missing a cause.

Every season, offices, clubs, pubs and family group chats run a footy tipping comp. People check the ladder on Monday, argue about a Thursday night upset, and give the winner a trophy nobody keeps. It works because it is small, regular and social — the same three things that make fundraising hard.

GoodTip keeps all of that and changes one thing at the end: the money lands somewhere good. Your organisation picks a charity, or puts it to a vote and lets the members decide. Everything raised goes there.

Tipping stays free for everyone playing. We are not trying to change how anybody spends their Friday — only where the pot ends up.

A word from our founder

Why does sport have to run on an industry that profits off people's addictions?

People think Good Tip is a weird move for me. They don't know rugby league's in my blood. My great uncle was Ray Stehr, a Rugby League Hall of Famer. He had a hard life and just got on with it. That's how my family's always done things.

Inequality is the thing that gets to me more than anything else. Good Tip is where I finally get to do something about it.

I've lost people most of my life, mates to drugs, to car accidents. Then 2024 happened. I lost my dad. I lost my brother-in-law to suicide. There was other stuff too that I won't get into here, but it was all the same year. I spent months just watching my family go through it.

Around then I was watching a lot of footy, mostly rugby league. It was the thing I'd switch on when I needed to switch off.

Except every ad break was a gambling ad. Odds read out during the commentary, betting logos everywhere on screen. And I remember thinking, this sport I love is being used to sell people the exact thing that just chewed up my family. Not the sport itself, obviously, but the industry riding on the back of it.

That's really where Good Tip started. Not as a plan, just as a question I couldn't shake: why does sport have to run on an industry that profits off people's addictions? What if it didn't have to?

Here's the other thing that got me. A workplace tipping comp already exists in most offices. Someone's always running the ladder. And whatever money's in it either goes to the winner or gets eaten by a bookmaker selling odds in between rounds. Nobody had built the third option.

I run a digital agency, specialising in search, so trust is more or less what I sell for a living. A few years ago one of my best people had a gambling problem, quietly, during work hours. He left not long after. Years later he came back, not for a job, but to send his own family's business my way. That's stayed with me.

So that's Good Tip. Your team tips every round, same as always. No entry fee, no prize pool, nobody's wage at risk. Your organisation pays one platform fee, and Good Tip funds a donation to whatever charity your group votes for.

I built it because I've watched what vulnerable actually looks like up close, and I've watched an industry cash in on it in broad daylight. If you've ever run an office comp and wondered where all that energy went, that's where it can go now.

Ian HopkinsonFounder & CEO

Where the money comes from

Nobody in the comp puts a dollar in.

This is the part people ask about first, so it is worth being plain about.

$
  1. 01

    Your organisation subscribes

    One platform fee for the season, paid by the organisation when the first round locks in. Not by the people tipping.

  2. 02

    GoodTip gives a share of it

    A share of that fee goes to the charity your organisation is backing. It comes out of our revenue, not out of anybody's pay packet.

  3. 03

    You get the receipt

    At the end of the season the Impact Report goes out: total raised, charity named, tipping results. One number your board can cite.

The bit that lasts

The comp is over by October. The money is still doing something in March.
